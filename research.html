BrainPrint: can brain signals help identify a person?

By Aounallah Meriem Sara and Chetti Chifaa · Workshop & Seminar, Artificial Intelligence Engineering

A research prototype, not a finished security system

Fingerprints, faces, irises and voices are all used to recognize people. Each has a weakness: they can be photographed, recorded or copied, and none of them can be changed after a leak. For this project we asked a different question: do brain signals contain enough information to tell people apart?

The idea

Electroencephalography (EEG) records tiny voltage differences on the scalp that come from the combined activity of many neurons. It does not read thoughts. People may show different patterns of activity, because their brains differ in structure, in the connections between neurons and in what they are doing mentally. We did not try to prove why the differences exist. We tested whether they are strong enough for a machine-learning model to use.

BrainPrint handles two tasks. Identification asks "who is this person?" among known people. Verification asks "is this really person X?", which needs a score for the claimed identity and a threshold.

The data

We used the public PhysioNet EEG dataset and selected a subset of participants.

How BrainPrint works

  1. Record. 64 scalp electrodes sample the signal 160 times per second.
  2. Filter. We keep 1 to 40 Hz to reduce slow drift and high-frequency noise. This is not full artifact removal, so eye blinks can remain.
  3. Split into windows. Each recording is cut into 4-second windows of 640 samples per channel.
  4. Extract features. For each channel we compute 5 absolute band powers, 5 relative band powers, the mean and the standard deviation. That gives 64 × 12 = 768 features per window.
  5. Classify. A Random Forest combines many decision trees and learns the patterns linked to each person.
  6. Decide. The model names an identity, or compares its score for a claimed identity with a threshold.

We trained on runs 1 to 6 and tested on runs 7 and 8. We never mixed windows from the same recording across training and testing, because that makes results look better than they are.

Results: identification

On unseen recordings, the model identified the right participant about 98% of the time, against 3.3% for random guessing among 30 people. Leaving one run out at a time gave a lower 89.4%, and results varied between runs.

How many electrodes do we need?

In a separate test on 10 participants, we used random subsets of electrodes. Select a size to see the accuracy.

95.3%

Eight random electrodes reached about 95%, so fewer sensors may be enough under controlled conditions. This was not a test of real low-cost headsets.

Which features carry the identity?

On the same 10 participants we removed groups of features. Using all features gave about 96%. Removing gamma and the temporal (ear) electrodes gave about 94%, and keeping only theta and alpha gave about 92%. No single group explains the result.

Results: verification

We enrolled 20 participants and treated 10 others as strangers, then averaged over 5 random splits. At a threshold of 0.30:

≈2.5%real users rejected (FRR)
≈1%enrolled impostors accepted (FAR)
≈2.9%unknown strangers accepted
≈2%equal error rate

Unknown strangers were harder to reject than enrolled impostors. We also built a small demo where you upload an EEG file, choose a claimed identity and move the threshold to see the score and the decision.

Recognizing the same person is not always easy. Accuracy drops sharply when mental state or recording conditions change.

Limits

All recordings for a participant come from one session, so unseen recordings are not independent sessions. Our results belong to this dataset and setup and do not prove long-term stability.

What we tested
  • 30 participants
  • Different recordings and task conditions
  • Identification and verification
  • Five random enrolled and stranger splits
  • Attempts from unknown strangers
What we did not test
  • Fatigue and emotion as controlled tests
  • Different EEG devices
  • Spoofing or replay attacks
  • Advanced artifact removal

Why brain data needs protection

Brain signals may reveal more than identity, and unlike a password they cannot simply be replaced after a leak. Because we have not tested spoofing, strong identification results alone do not prove security.

What comes next

  • Test lower-cost EEG headsets.
  • Explore revocable mental passwords, where a mental task acts as a changeable key.
  • Compare Random Forest with deep learning, such as convolutional neural networks.
  • Reduce the number of electrodes.

Conclusion. EEG signals can help recognize people under controlled conditions. More tests are needed across different days, mental states and devices, and privacy and practical use remain open challenges.

Data source: PhysioNet EEG datasets, physionet.org
